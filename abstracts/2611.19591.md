# Extracting Damping Ratio and Shear Wave Velocity through Joint Inversion of Rayleigh Wave Attenuation and Phase Velocity Data

**Authors:** Mrinal Bhaumik, Brady Cox

Methods for reliable in-situ estimation of small-strain damping ratio remain far less developed than those for in-situ shear wave velocity (Vs) measurements. For surface wave-based approaches, challenges with extracting damping ratios are caused by both difficulties associated with developing attenuation coefficients from recorded wavefields and complexities involved when interpreting their multimodal irregularities during inversion. This study presents a joint inversion framework for extracting shear damping ratio (Ds0) and Vs profiles from Rayleigh-wave attenuation and phase velocity data using a viscoelastic predominant-mode approach. Unlike conventional inversion, the proposed formulation identifies the mode contributing most strongly to the measured wavefield, allowing multimodal attenuation and dispersion data to be incorporated without explicit modal indexing. This approach has significant benefits for inverting attenuation data, whose modal behavior is much more irregular than phase velocity. A coupled inversion strategy is adopted to jointly estimate the Vs and Ds0 while addressing their different sensitivities to phase velocity and attenuation. The predominant-mode attenuation concept is first validated against two synthetic ground models of increasing complexity. The synthetic inversion results demonstrate the framework's ability to recover the Ds0 and Vs profiles and capture modal transitions associated with a low-velocity layer. The framework is then applied to active-source field data from Mirandola, Italy, where the fundamental mode dominates, and Bird's Landing, California, where a soft peat layer produces significant higher-modes. At both sites, the inverted Vs profiles agreed well with independent site characterization data, while the damping estimates exhibit physically plausible trends and provide site-specific information that complements empirical estimates.
